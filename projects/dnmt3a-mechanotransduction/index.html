DNMT3A mechanotransduction during cutaneous wound repair

This project asks how wound-induced physical cues are translated into nuclear decisions during epidermal repair, with a focus on the relocalization of the de novo DNA methyltransferase DNMT3A.

Overview

Why this project matters

Cutaneous injury is not only a biochemical event. Wounds reshape tissue tension, cytoskeletal organization, and the physical environment experienced by epidermal cells. This project is centered on how those mechanical changes become durable molecular signals.

In particular, the work examines how wound-associated mechanics regulate the nucleocytoplasmic partitioning of DNMT3A, linking actin-dependent mechanotransduction to an epigenetic regulator with the potential to influence repair-associated cell-state change.

Program fit

This is the clearest example in my portfolio of how mechanical context, nuclear signaling, and chromatin regulation converge during tissue repair.

Research setting

Developed in the Jamora laboratory as part of a broader research program in skin biology, wound healing, and regenerative cell-state transitions.

Core Questions

How the project is framed

What changes at the wound edge?

The project starts from the idea that injury alters cytoskeletal and physical conditions in ways that may actively instruct epidermal cell behavior.

How is a nuclear regulator repositioned?

A central question is how actin remodeling and ERK1/2 signaling help regulate the subcellular localization of DNMT3A during the repair response.

Why does localization matter?

The broader implication is that mechanotransduction may shape epigenetic decision-making and thereby influence repair-associated cell-state transitions.

Approach

Experimental frame

The project integrates molecular biology, biochemistry, fluorescence microscopy, and mechanobiology-oriented analysis to connect wound-associated physical cues with the localization and function of DNMT3A.

The work is built around epidermal systems in which cytoskeletal perturbation, signaling behavior, and nuclear relocalization can be interpreted together rather than in isolation. That framing is important because the biological question is not just whether DNMT3A moves, but what kind of wound-state logic is being encoded when it does.

Methods and readouts

  • Primary keratinocyte-focused analysis
  • Fluorescence microscopy of subcellular localization
  • Signaling perturbation and mechanotransduction context
  • Integration of cell-biological and molecular readouts
